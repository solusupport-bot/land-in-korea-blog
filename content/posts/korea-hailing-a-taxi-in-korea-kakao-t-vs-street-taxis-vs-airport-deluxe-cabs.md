Standing on a curb in Korea with your arm out is not the default anymore. It works — but you're doing it the way most Koreans stopped doing it years ago. The bigger problem isn't catching a taxi; it's explaining where you want to go once you're inside.

## The real friction point is the address, not the car

Korean addresses are not fun to pronounce under pressure, and "the hotel near the big intersection" gets you nowhere. This is the single reason Kakao T dominates: you set your pickup and drop-off on a map, and the destination arrives on the driver's screen already written in Korean. No charades, no showing a screenshot at a red light.

The app works with a foreign phone number in most cases. You can register a card for automatic payment, or just pay the driver at the end like a normal taxi ride. Either way, the awkward part of the trip is already solved before the car arrives.

One dependency worth naming: Kakao T needs data. If you're arriving without a working connection, sort that out at the airport before you need your first ride — a [prepaid Korean SIM or eSIM](({{klook}})) is the cheap insurance here.

## When street hailing still beats the app

In busy commercial areas during normal hours, sticking your hand out is genuinely fine and often faster than waiting for a match. Empty taxis are everywhere.

Where it falls apart is late night in nightlife districts. You're competing with a crowd of people doing exactly the same thing, and drivers sometimes choose the passenger heading in the direction they already want to go. That's the scenario where the app quietly wins — your destination is locked in before anyone decides whether they like it.

## Not all taxis cost the same

This surprises people at the airport. There are tiers:

- **Standard taxis** — usually orange, silver or white in Seoul. The cheapest option and the default.
- **Deluxe taxis (모범택시)** — black, with a yellow sign on the roof. Noticeably higher fares. They cluster at airports and hotel driveways, which is exactly where a tired visitor grabs the first car in line.
- **Jumbo vans** — for larger groups or a lot of luggage.

If you land at the airport and take the nearest black cab without checking, you've chosen the premium tier by accident. Sometimes that's worth it for space and comfort. Just make it a decision rather than a default.

## Fares, surcharges and paying

A late-night surcharge applies during the small hours. Rates are set locally, so fares differ slightly between cities and provinces — and a taxi taking you outside its licensed area may add an extra charge on top. Ask or expect it if you're heading well out of town.

Payment is easy: almost every taxi accepts cards and T-money. Tipping is not expected anywhere in Korea, full stop. Don't round up, don't leave change on the seat — it just causes confusion.

## The practical takeaway

Install Kakao T before you arrive and make sure you have data. Use the app for anything late at night, anything with a complicated destination, and anything outside the city centre. Flag a taxi on the street when you're somewhere busy and the destination is simple. And at the airport, glance at the roof sign before you climb in.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use Kakao T with a foreign phone number?

In most cases, yes — the app works with a foreign phone number. You can register a card for automatic payment or simply pay the driver directly at the end of the ride.

### Do I need to tip taxi drivers in Korea?

No. Tipping is not expected anywhere in Korea, and almost every taxi accepts cards and T-money for payment.

### Why are the black taxis at the airport more expensive?

Those are deluxe taxis (모범택시), identifiable by the yellow sign on the roof, and they charge noticeably higher fares than standard orange, silver or white taxis. They're common at airports and hotels, so it's easy to take one without realising it's the premium tier.
