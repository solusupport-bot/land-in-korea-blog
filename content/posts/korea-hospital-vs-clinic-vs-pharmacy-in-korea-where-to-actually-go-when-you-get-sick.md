## The short version

Large hospitals often expect a referral for non-emergency visits and charge more without one. For a cold, stomach bug, or skin rash, the clinic on the second floor of a random commercial building is the right call.

Pharmacies (약국, yakguk) are separate from clinics in Korea — doctors write a prescription and you fill it at a nearby pharmacy, almost always within a minute's walk. Pharmacists can also recommend over-the-counter medicine directly for mild symptoms, and many are used to gesturing and simple English. Without Korean national health insurance you'll pay the full uninsured rate, which for a basic clinic visit is still far cheaper than in many Western countries, but travel insurance receipts matter — ask for an itemized receipt and diagnosis certificate before leaving.

## Reading a clinic's specialty from the sign

Korean clinic names almost always end in a suffix that tells you what they treat, which matters because most clinics specialize narrowly rather than offering general practice the way a Western family doctor does. 내과 (naegwa) is internal medicine — the closest thing to a general doctor, and the right default for a cold, fever, or stomach issue. 이비인후과 (ibiinhugwa) is ENT, for throat, ear, and sinus problems. 피부과 (pibugwa) is dermatology, 정형외과 (jeonghyeongoegwa) is orthopedics for sprains and joint pain, 안과 (angwa) is ophthalmology, and 치과 (chigwa) is dentistry. Clinics cluster together in commercial buildings near subway exits, often stacked floor by floor, so spotting the right suffix on a building directory saves a wasted trip.

## When to call 119 instead of walking in

Korea's 119 is a free ambulance and fire line, reachable from any phone including one without a Korean SIM, and dispatchers can connect an English interpreter if needed — use it for anything genuinely urgent (chest pain, serious injury, loss of consciousness) rather than trying to taxi to a hospital yourself. For something that's clearly not an emergency but can't wait for a clinic's hours, a hospital's emergency department (응급실, eungeupsil) will see walk-ins, but expect a longer wait and a higher bill than a same-day clinic visit would have cost, since the emergency department is built around triage by severity, not arrival order.

## Clinics for children, and what NHIS coverage changes

Pediatric clinics follow the same suffix logic — look for 소아과 (soagwa) specifically rather than a general 내과, since staff and waiting rooms there are set up for kids. For longer-term residents enrolled in Korea's National Health Insurance Service (NHIS), the cost difference at any of these tiers drops sharply: insured patients pay a fraction of the uninsured rate at clinics and pharmacies, which is part of why NHIS enrollment matters quickly once you're working or studying in Korea rather than something to delay. Bring the physical NHIS card or have the clinic look up your enrollment by your ARC number — reception staff handle this daily and it adds only a minute to check-in.

## Dermatology and dentistry see a lot of foreign patients already

피부과 (dermatology) and 치과 (dentistry) clinics, especially in Seoul's Gangnam and Myeongdong areas, handle enough international patients — including dedicated medical tourism — that English-language service, online booking, and card payment for uninsured visitors tend to be smoother there than at a random neighborhood clinic. That doesn't mean every dermatology or dental clinic is foreigner-oriented, but it's a reasonable place to start searching if the issue isn't urgent and you have the flexibility to pick a clinic rather than use the nearest one.

## Saturday hours are shorter, not absent

Most neighborhood clinics open a reduced Saturday schedule — often just the morning — rather than closing entirely, which matters for planning around a weekend trip. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the most common first-timer mistake with hospital vs. clinic vs. pharmacy in korea: where to actually go when you get sick?

Korea's medical system is tiered, and walking into a big university hospital for a sore throat is usually the slowest and most expensive choice.

### What should I know before I go?

Small neighborhood clinics — look for signs ending in 의원 (uiwon) with a specialty like 내과 (internal medicine), 이비인후과 (ENT), or 피부과 (dermatology) — handle everyday illness, generally take walk-ins, and see you quickly.

### Any quick tip to remember?

Note that many clinics close for a lunch hour and are shut on Sundays and holidays.
