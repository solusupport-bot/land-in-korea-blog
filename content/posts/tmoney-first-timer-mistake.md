You already know T-money exists: tap in, tap out, works on subway/bus/taxi. That's not the part that trips people up. The part that actually costs money is this:

## The mistake: not tapping out on a bus

On the subway, skipping the tap-out is obvious — the gate won't open. On a bus, nothing stops you from just walking off without tapping again. Nothing beeps, no one checks. But the transfer discount (subway ↔ bus, or bus ↔ bus, within the transfer window, typically around 30 minutes) only applies if you tap out — so skip it and you silently pay full fare on your *next* ride instead of the discounted transfer rate, with zero indication anything went wrong.

Multiply that by every bus ride on a week-long trip and it's a real, avoidable cost — not because the fare is expensive, but because it happens without you noticing, ride after ride.

## The habit that fixes it

Tap out at the front or back door reader every single time you get off a bus, even when it feels unnecessary. That's it — this alone is the difference between paying transfer rates and paying full fare on every connecting ride for your entire trip.

## It's not just a transit card

T-money doubles as a general prepaid payment card at a huge number of convenience stores across the country — CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, and others all accept a tap of the same card for small purchases, which is genuinely convenient when your hands are full or you just don't want to dig for cash. It also works at many vending machines and coin lockers. If you're already relying on convenience stores for quick meals and snacks, our [convenience store guide](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-convenience-store-guide.html) covers more of what these stores are actually useful for beyond snacks.

## T-money isn't the only card

T-money is the most recognized brand, but it isn't the only one — Cashbee is a competing transit card brand that works interchangeably on most of the same subway and bus systems, so if a store or kiosk happens to only stock Cashbee, it'll still work the same way. On the mobile side, some Korean Android phones support a mobile version of the card built into the phone's NFC chip, but this isn't something a visiting foreign traveler can easily set up mid-trip regardless of phone brand — the physical card remains the reliable, no-setup option for visitors, iPhone or Android.

## Does it work outside Seoul?

Mostly, yes — T-money works on public transit in most major Korean cities, not just Seoul, which is part of why it's worth buying even if Seoul isn't your only stop. That said, a handful of smaller regional bus systems run their own separate card systems with limited or no interoperability, so if your itinerary includes a smaller city or a rural bus route, it's worth a quick check locally rather than assuming your Seoul-bought card will work everywhere without exception.

## One more thing nobody mentions

Unused T-money balance can be partially refunded before you leave Korea, in cash, at station customer service counters — but not all stations offer this, and it usually excludes the original card fee. If you're topping up near the end of your trip, top up in smaller amounts rather than one large recharge, so you're not leaving money on a card you're about to throw away.

## Where to get a card if you haven't yet

T-money cards are sold at convenience stores and station kiosks on arrival — no need to pre-book, and basic cards cost only a few thousand won plus whatever balance you load. Branded or character-design cards are also sold as souvenirs at a higher price but work exactly the same way as the plain version. If you'd rather sort out transit before you land, Klook and Trip.com also list Korea transit card bundles.

- Cashbee is an interchangeable alternative brand if that's what a kiosk has in stock
