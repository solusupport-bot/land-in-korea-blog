Jjimjilbang guides tend to either oversell the awkwardness or undersell the one rule that actually matters. The real answer is that a Korean bathhouse runs two completely different dress codes in two adjacent areas, and mixing them up is the only way to actually cause a scene.

## Two zones, two completely different rules

A jjimjilbang is really two facilities sharing one building. The **bathing area** (the wet baths, showers, and pools) is strictly gender-separated and nudity is required — not optional, not a stall option, an actual rule enforced by the layout itself, since there are no partial-privacy options there. The **common area** (heated kiln saunas, the lounge, the sleeping areas) is co-ed, and everyone wears the matching uniform t-shirt and shorts provided at check-in. Walking into the wrong zone in the wrong state of dress is the actual etiquette failure — not nudity itself, which is completely normal and expected on the bathing side.

## Nudity in the bath area is a hygiene rule, not a big deal

For first-timers, the nudity requirement is usually the biggest source of anxiety going in, and it helps to know it's treated as a plumbing and hygiene standard, not a performance or a test of comfort. Everyone in that section — regardless of age, body type, or how long they've been coming — is nude, and no one is paying attention to anyone else in the way a Western sauna or gym locker room might condition you to expect. Bringing or wearing swimwear into the actual bathing pools is generally not permitted; it defeats the sanitation logic the separation is built on.

## Tattoos are mostly fine now, but "mostly" is doing some work

Korean attitudes toward tattoos in bathhouses have shifted considerably — most modern jjimjilbangs, especially larger or foreigner-frequented ones, don't restrict visitors with tattoos, and it's genuinely common to see them without any issue. That said, policy isn't universal: some older or more conservative establishments still discourage or restrict visible tattoos, particularly larger ones. If you have significant visible tattoos and you're set on a specific location, a quick call ahead or checking recent reviews avoids an awkward turn-away at the door.

## What it actually costs and how long you can stay

Entry typically runs somewhere in the ₩10,000–20,000 range for access lasting up to 12 hours, which is part of why jjimjilbangs double as a legitimate budget overnight option in Korea — some travelers use one intentionally after a late flight or an early departure rather than booking a hotel for a few hours. That price usually includes the uniform, locker access, and the common sauna areas; food and drink at the in-house snack counter (an actual jjimjilbang tradition, not an afterthought) is separate.

## What check-in actually looks like

Entry follows a fairly consistent pattern regardless of location: pay at the front counter, receive a locker key (often built into a wristband, since pockets aren't really a thing once you're in the uniform), and remove your shoes at the entrance into a separate shoe locker before reaching the main change area. Shoes and street clothes never go past that first threshold — shoe lockers use a different key from your main locker, so keep both straight. From there, the bathing area and the common area are usually clearly separated by a doorway or a change in flooring, which is the practical marker for which dress code applies on which side.

## Where foreigner-friendly locations actually help

Some jjimjilbangs, particularly ones in areas like Itaewon that see more foreign visitors, are set up with English signage and staff more used to explaining the system to first-timers. That doesn't change any of the underlying rules — nudity in the bath area and uniforms in the common area apply everywhere — but it does mean less guessing about lockers, towel stations, and where the entrance to each zone actually is on your first visit.

## What to actually bring

Most jjimjilbangs supply a towel, the uniform, and basic toiletries as part of entry, so you don't need to pack much. Bringing your own hair tie, a phone-safe waterproof pouch if you plan to bring your phone into the common area, and a change of underwear for after covers most of what regulars actually carry — everything else genuinely is provided.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Only in the gender-separated bathing area, where nudity is required as a hygiene standard. In the co-ed common areas (saunas, lounge), everyone wears the provided uniform instead.

### Are tattoos allowed in Korean bathhouses?

Mostly yes at modern locations, and attitudes have shifted a lot — but some older or more conservative jjimjilbangs still restrict visible tattoos, so it's worth checking ahead if you have larger ones and a specific location in mind.

### Can I wear a swimsuit in the jjimjilbang bathing pools?

Generally no — swimwear isn't permitted in the actual bathing pools, since the nudity-based system there is built around a specific hygiene standard.

### How much does a jjimjilbang cost and how long can I stay?

Typically ₩10,000–20,000 for access lasting up to 12 hours, which is why some travelers use one as a legitimate budget overnight option.

### Are jjimjilbangs mixed-gender?

The bathing areas are strictly separated by gender. The common sauna and lounge areas are co-ed, but everyone wears the same uniform there.

### What should I bring to a jjimjilbang?

Very little — towels, the uniform, and basic toiletries are usually included in the entry fee. A hair tie and a waterproof phone pouch cover most of what regulars bring extra.
