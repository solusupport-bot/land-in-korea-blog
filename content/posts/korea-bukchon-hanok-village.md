## It's a neighborhood, not an exhibit

The single most common mistake first-time visitors make at Bukchon Hanok Village is treating it like an open-air museum. It isn't one — real families live behind those tiled roofs, in houses that have stood for generations. That changes how you should behave while you're there, not just what you should photograph.

## Why some alleys post signs

Because this is a lived-in residential area, some of the narrower alleys post "quiet please" signs, particularly in the early morning and evening hours when residents are home. Keeping your voice down and being mindful of camera angles into windows and doorways isn't just polite — it's the baseline expectation for visiting a real neighborhood.

## Timing the famous photo spot

The sloped alley view near Gahoe-dong is the shot most people have seen online, and it gets crowded fast once tour groups arrive mid-morning. Going early on a weekday is the difference between an empty frame and a line of other visitors doing the same thing.

## It connects two palaces

Bukchon sits between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung, which is why most visitors treat it as a walking connector between the two rather than a standalone destination. If you're already doing one palace, adding Bukchon on the way to (or from) the other costs very little extra time.

## The short version

- It's a real, lived-in neighborhood — not a museum
- Some alleys have posted quiet hours out of respect for residents
- The Gahoe-dong photo spot is best early on a weekday
- It sits conveniently between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung
