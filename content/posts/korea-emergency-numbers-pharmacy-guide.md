112 (police) and 119 (fire, ambulance, medical emergencies) both have English-language phone support in Korea, and most travel guides stop there. But most of what actually goes wrong on a trip isn't a 112-or-119 situation — it's smaller than that, and there's a better number for it.

## What 112 and 119 are each actually for

It's worth being clear on the split, since calling the wrong one wastes time in an actual emergency: **112** is for police — crime, theft, an unsafe situation involving another person. **119** covers fire, ambulance dispatch, and medical emergencies — call this one for a health crisis, not 112. Both can be reached free from any phone, and both offer interpretation support for non-Korean speakers, though response can be faster if you're able to say a few basic words in Korean (your location especially) before the interpreter connects.

## The number that actually solves most travel problems: 1330

The 1330 Korea Travel Hotline is a free, 24/7 multilingual line built specifically for travel-related issues — needing an interpreter at a hospital, a scam dispute, or just not knowing who to call. Save it before 112 and 119, since it's the one you'll actually use for anything short of a genuine emergency. One limit worth knowing: 1330 can help you navigate a lost passport situation and interpret for you, but it can't issue you a new one — an actual passport replacement or emergency travel document still has to come from your own country's embassy or consulate in Korea, so it's worth saving that contact information too, before you need it rather than while panicking without it.

## Where to go for real medical care

For anything beyond a pharmacy visit, large hospitals in Seoul — Severance Hospital and Samsung Medical Center among them — run dedicated international clinics with English-speaking staff. Worth knowing the name of one near where you're staying before you need it, not after.

## The 24-hour assumption that's usually wrong

[24-hour convenience stores](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-convenience-store-guide.html) are everywhere in Korea. 24-hour pharmacies are not — they're far less common, and most travelers only discover this at the worst possible time. Look for a green cross sign, and if you can't find one open, ask hotel front desk staff — they'll usually know the nearest late-night option.

## Two small things that make a pharmacy visit faster

Over-the-counter medicine names differ from Western brands, so showing the pharmacist a translated symptom list ([Papago's camera translation](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-travel-apps-comparison.html) works well for this) speeds things up considerably. And if you don't already have travel insurance, get it before you land — Korean hospitals and clinics generally require upfront payment at the point of care, with reimbursement handled afterward through your own insurer rather than billed directly to it, which catches visitors used to a different system off guard.

## Before you need any of this

A few minutes of prep before your trip makes all of the above faster if you ever need it: save your embassy or consulate's Seoul contact information in your phone, keep a translated note of any serious allergies or existing medical conditions and your blood type, and keep your travel insurance policy number somewhere accessible offline, not only in an email you might not be able to load in a hurry.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What number should travelers actually call first for a travel problem?

1330, the Korea Travel Hotline — a free, 24/7 multilingual line for interpreters, scam disputes, and general travel issues, not just genuine emergencies like 112 or 119.

### What's the difference between 112 and 119?

112 is for police matters — crime or an unsafe situation. 119 covers fire, ambulance dispatch, and medical emergencies. Calling the right one first matters in a real emergency.

### Can the 1330 hotline replace my lost passport?

No — it can help you navigate the situation and interpret, but issuing a replacement or emergency travel document has to come from your own country's embassy or consulate in Korea.

### Are pharmacies open 24 hours in Korea?

Not usually — unlike convenience stores, 24-hour pharmacies are uncommon. Look for a green cross sign or ask your hotel front desk for the nearest late-night option.

### What should I bring to a Korean pharmacy?

A translated symptom list (Papago's camera translation works well), since over-the-counter medicine names differ from Western brands.

### Do Korean hospitals bill my travel insurance directly?

Usually not — you typically pay upfront at the point of care and file for reimbursement with your own insurer afterward, rather than the hospital billing your insurance directly.
