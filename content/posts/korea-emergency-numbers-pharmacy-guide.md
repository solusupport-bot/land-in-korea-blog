112 (police) and 119 (fire, ambulance, medical emergencies) both have English-language phone support in Korea, and most travel guides stop there. But most of what actually goes wrong on a trip isn't a 112-or-119 situation — it's smaller than that, and there's a better number for it.

## The number that actually solves most travel problems: 1330

The 1330 Korea Travel Hotline is a free, 24/7 multilingual line built specifically for travel-related issues — a lost passport, needing an interpreter at a hospital, a scam dispute, or just not knowing who to call. Save it before 112 and 119, since it's the one you'll actually use for anything short of a genuine emergency.

## Where to go for real medical care

For anything beyond a pharmacy visit, large hospitals in Seoul — Severance Hospital and Samsung Medical Center among them — run dedicated international clinics with English-speaking staff. Worth knowing the name of one near where you're staying before you need it, not after.

## The 24-hour assumption that's usually wrong

24-hour convenience stores are everywhere in Korea. 24-hour pharmacies are not — they're far less common, and most travelers only discover this at the worst possible time. Look for a green cross sign, and if you can't find one open, ask hotel front desk staff — they'll usually know the nearest late-night option.

## Two small things that make a pharmacy visit faster

Over-the-counter medicine names differ from Western brands, so showing the pharmacist a translated symptom list (Papago's camera translation works well for this) speeds things up considerably. And if you don't already have travel insurance, get it before you land — Korean hospitals generally require upfront payment, with reimbursement handled afterward through your insurer, not at the point of care.
