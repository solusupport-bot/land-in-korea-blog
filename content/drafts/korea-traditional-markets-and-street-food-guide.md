Korean traditional markets (jaerae sijang) are where a lot of first-time visitors have their best meal of the trip — and also where the most avoidable mistakes happen, mostly around cash, ordering, and picking a market that doesn't match what they actually wanted. This guide is about getting that right the first time.

## Pick the market based on what you want, not which one is "best"

There's no single best market — it depends on whether you're there for food or for shopping.

**For food: Gwangjang Market.** Opened in 1905, it's Korea's oldest permanent market, and its second-floor food alley is purpose-built for visitors who want to eat, not browse. It was featured on Netflix's Street Food: Asia, which is part of why it's now busy with both locals and tourists at peak lunch hours. Nearest subway: Jongno 5-ga Station (Line 1), Exit 8. Hours run roughly 9:00 AM to 11:00 PM, though individual stalls vary.

**For shopping, with food as a side stop: Namdaemun Market.** It's Korea's largest traditional market by size, and some sections stay open very late or around the clock. Food here (hotteok, kalguksu) is real but secondary to the clothing, household goods, and import-shop stalls that make up most of the market. If you want the fuller comparison between Namdaemun and the nearby Myeongdong shopping district, see our [Namdaemun Market guide](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-namdaemun-market.html).

If you're only doing one market and you're undecided: pick Gwangjang if food is the goal, Namdaemun if shopping is.

## What to actually order at Gwangjang

Walking into a food alley with no plan is how people end up pointing at random and overpaying. These are the dishes locals and repeat visitors specifically go back for:

- **Bindaetteok** — a thick, savory mung bean pancake, pan-fried. The market's signature dish.
- **Mayak gimbap** — small, bite-sized seaweed rice rolls, named "narcotic gimbap" half-jokingly because people reportedly can't stop eating them.
- **Yukhoe** — Korean-style raw beef tartare, seasoned rather than sauced like Western steak tartare. Order this from a stall that's visibly busy with turnover, not one with raw beef sitting out unattended.
- **Kalguksu** — hand-cut knife noodles in broth, a good warm option outside of summer.

A reasonable approach for a first visit: split 2-3 dishes between two people rather than ordering one full dish each, since portions at food-alley stalls are usually meant for sharing or grazing across several stalls.

## Payment: bring cash

This is the single most common first-timer mistake. Many market stalls, including well-known food stalls at both Gwangjang and Namdaemun, take cash only — no cards, and often no mobile pay either. Withdraw cash before you go (ATMs at major subway stations work reliably with foreign cards) rather than discovering this standing at a stall with a line behind you.

If a stall does take cards, it will usually have a visible card reader or a sign — don't assume.

## Market etiquette that actually matters

- **Don't photograph vendors or their stalls up close without asking.** A quick gesture and a nod is enough — most vendors are fine with it, but asking first avoids the occasional sharp response.
- **Don't block narrow aisles to take a photo.** Gwangjang's food alley in particular gets genuinely tight at peak hours; step to the side.
- **Haggling is for goods, not food.** It's normal (if modest) at Namdaemun's clothing and goods stalls. It's not a thing at food stalls — the price on the sign or spoken by the vendor is the price.
- **Tipping is not expected or practiced** at either market. See our [tipping in Korea guide](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-tipping-and-service-charges.html) if you want the fuller explanation of why.

## When to go

Both markets are busiest at lunch (roughly 12-1:30 PM) and, for Gwangjang specifically, in the early evening once it shows up in more people's "what to do tonight" plans. If you want a calmer walk-through with shorter lines, aim for a 10-11 AM arrival, after stalls have opened but before the lunch rush.

## Getting there and getting around after

Both markets are accessible by subway, and a [T-money card](https://landinkorea.com/posts/korea-t-money-transit-card.html) covers the fare without needing a paper ticket. Market hours and general practical details are checked against [Korea Tourism Organization](https://english.visitkorea.or.kr) and [Seoul Tourism Organization](https://www.seoultourism.org/seoul-traditional-markets/). ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is street food at these markets safe for visitors?

Yes — these are high-turnover stalls serving large volumes of food daily, which generally means fresher ingredients, not riskier ones. The usual common-sense rule applies: pick stalls with visible through-traffic over ones sitting empty.

### Can I use a credit card anywhere in these markets?

Some stalls can, most can't. Don't rely on cards — bring cash as your primary payment method and treat card acceptance as a bonus where you find it.

### Is Gwangjang Market too touristy now to be worth it?

It's busier than it used to be, partly from international food-show coverage, but the food quality and the format (small shared dishes across multiple stalls) haven't changed. It's busy with a mix of locals and visitors, not purely a tourist trap — the stalls that have run for decades are still run by the same families serving the same dishes.

### What if I have dietary restrictions?

Vegetarian options are limited at both markets — most of the well-known dishes involve meat or seafood-based broths. Bindaetteok (the mung bean pancake) is usually vegetarian-friendly, but confirm with the vendor since some versions include pork.
