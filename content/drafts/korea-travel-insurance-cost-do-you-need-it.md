Travel insurance isn't a Korean entry requirement for most short-term tourist visits — you won't be asked for proof at immigration the way you might for some other countries. That doesn't mean it's not worth having. The real question is what it actually costs and whether the coverage matches what you're likely to need.

**Note on pricing below:** these figures come from insurance comparison sites and sellers, accessed 2026-10-31, not a single authoritative source — insurance pricing depends heavily on your age, home country, trip length, and coverage level, so treat these as starting ranges to compare against your own quote, not a fixed price.

## Do you actually need it?

Not legally, for most visitors on a standard tourist stay. But two practical facts make it worth considering anyway:

1. **Your home insurance probably doesn't cover you here.** US health insurance and Medicare, for example, generally don't extend to care received in South Korea — a point multiple sources flagged specifically. If you're not sure whether your home policy covers international medical care, assume it doesn't until you've confirmed otherwise.
2. **Korean hospital care isn't free for foreign tourists, and a single ER visit adds up.** One source puts a single emergency visit at 500,000 won or more (roughly $370+) before any treatment, imaging, or admission costs are added.

## What it actually costs

Estimates vary more than most price comparisons on this site, because "travel insurance" bundles several different kinds of coverage that get priced separately:

**Medical and emergency cover (a 1-2 week trip):** One source gives basic medical/emergency-only cover at $15-35, with more comprehensive cover at $40-80 for the same trip length. Another source estimates roughly $10/day as a simple baseline.

**As a percentage of trip cost:** A separate source puts typical South Korea trip insurance at 4-8% of your total trip cost — useful as a sanity check if a quote seems unusually high or low relative to how much the trip itself costs.

**Trip-cancellation cover:** This is the category where age matters most. One insurer's own published table, for a 15-day trip with a $1,000 insured trip cost, shows roughly $24 for a 20-year-old traveler on its cheapest plan, rising to roughly $74 for a 70-year-old on the same plan. If you're booking for an older traveler or a multi-generation family trip, expect real variation in the cancellation-cover line item by traveler age.

**Group/tour insurance:** At least one tour operator budgets separately for trip insurance at roughly $150 per person on a guided group tour — higher than the self-booked individual estimates above, likely reflecting a higher coverage tier bundled by default.

## What to actually check in a policy, not just the price

- **Medical coverage limit.** Confirm it's high enough to cover a real hospital stay, not just a clinic visit — Korean hospital care is good but not free, and a multi-day admission costs meaningfully more than the ER-visit figure above.
- **Adventure-activity exclusions.** Several sources specifically flag this: if you plan to hike Hallasan on Jeju, ski in the PyeongChang area, or do anything classified as an "adventure activity," check whether your policy covers it by default or needs an add-on. Standard policies frequently exclude this.
- **Trip-delay coverage.** Worth checking specifically if your trip includes Jeju — flights and ferries there are weather-vulnerable, and trip-delay cover is what actually pays out if a storm cancels your inter-island hop, not standard trip-cancellation cover.
- **Pre-existing conditions.** As with most travel insurance globally, pre-existing condition coverage usually requires either an add-on or a policy specifically marketed as covering it — check this before assuming you're covered.
- **How claims actually work from Korea.** Confirm whether the insurer has English-language claims support and whether you pay upfront and get reimbursed (common at Korean hospitals for foreign visitors without local insurance) or whether the insurer coordinates directly with the hospital.

## A simple way to decide

If your trip is short (under a week), you're young and healthy, and your home insurance has some international component, you might reasonably decide the medical-only risk is manageable without buying a separate policy — that's a real judgment call, not something this guide can make for you. If any of the following apply, insurance is worth the relatively small cost: longer trip, older traveler, planned adventure activities (hiking, skiing), a Jeju leg vulnerable to weather delay, or a non-refundable trip cost large enough that cancellation would genuinely hurt.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is travel insurance legally required to enter Korea?

No, not for most short-term tourist visits as of this writing. Requirements can change, so if you're applying for a long-stay visa type rather than a standard tourist entry, check that visa category's specific requirements separately.

### Does my credit card's built-in travel insurance cover this?

Possibly, but check the specific terms — many card-provided policies have lower medical limits than a dedicated travel policy and may exclude adventure activities by default, same as standalone policies. Don't assume card coverage is equivalent without reading the actual policy document.

### What's the single most important thing to check if I only check one thing?

The medical coverage limit. A $15-35 basic plan with a low medical cap is a different product from a $40-80 comprehensive plan with a high one — for the actual risk (an unexpected hospital stay), the coverage ceiling matters more than the daily premium difference.

### Can I buy travel insurance after I've already arrived in Korea?

Some providers allow this, but many travel-specific policies (especially trip-cancellation cover) only make sense or are only available if purchased before departure. Buy before you leave if cancellation or pre-trip coverage matters to you.
