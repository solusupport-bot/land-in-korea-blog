Most people arriving in Korea for two months book a hotel for the first week and panic-search Airbnb for the rest. That's the expensive mistake. The gap between "tourist stay" and "signed a one-year lease" is exactly where Korea has options almost no first-timer knows about — and they cost a fraction of what you're about to pay.

The week-to-a-few-months gap: goshiwons and share houses

A goshiwon is the option that surprises people most. It's a very small private room rented by the month, with no deposit and utilities included in the price. They were originally built for students cramming for civil service and bar exams, which tells you everything about the design philosophy: a bed, a desk, and not much else. Many rooms are windowless and genuinely tight.

The trade-off is real, though. No deposit means no lump sum to scrape together, and no long contract means you can move in almost immediately. For someone doing a two-month language course or bridging a job start date, that's hard to beat.

Share houses and monthly-rent guesthouses sit a step above. You get a private or shared room, a lighter contract than a standard lease, and usually a common kitchen and living area. More space, more social, more money.

What about short-term Airbnb-style rentals? The legal picture is messier than the listings suggest. Only certain registered categories are clearly permitted — urban homestays licensed specifically to host foreign guests, for example. Unregistered listings can and do get cancelled, sometimes late. If you book one, have a backup.

Actual leases: wolse, jeonse, and why the deposit is the whole story

Once you're signing a real lease, Korea runs on two systems.

Wolse is monthly rent plus a security deposit called the bojeunggeum. Note that "security deposit" undersells it — by international standards this is typically a large lump sum, not one month's rent.

Jeonse is the one that confuses everyone: no monthly rent at all. Instead you hand over a very large deposit, which is returned in full when you leave. The landlord profits from holding and investing that money rather than from rent.

Because these deposits are so large, tenant protection isn't optional paperwork — it's the entire point.

Protecting your deposit: the non-negotiables

  • Register your lease and move-in date at the local district office. This is what secures your priority claim on your deposit. Do it immediately, not eventually.
  • Check the property's registry document before signing. You're looking for existing mortgages on the property. A heavily mortgaged building means your deposit sits behind the bank in line.
  • Verify the landlord's name matches the registry. Sounds obvious. It's the check that catches the most problems.
  • Go through a licensed agent. Agent commission is set by a legal scale based on the contract value and is paid by both sides, so you know roughly what you're in for.

Jeonse deposit-return fraud has been a real and widely reported problem in Korea — not a rare edge case. That's precisely why first-time renters are steered toward licensed agents and registry checks rather than a handshake and a good feeling about someone.

The practical takeaway

If you're here under three months, don't sign a lease. Take a goshiwon or share house, live somewhere real, and learn which neighbourhood you actually want. If you're staying longer, budget for the deposit shock early — and treat the district office registration and registry check as the first two things you do, not the last.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a deposit for a goshiwon in Korea?

No — a goshiwon is rented by the month with no deposit, and utilities are included in the price. That's what makes it one of the fastest options to move into.

What's the difference between jeonse and wolse?

Wolse is monthly rent plus a security deposit (bojeunggeum), while jeonse means you pay no monthly rent at all but hand over a very large deposit that's returned when you leave.

How do I protect my deposit when renting in Korea?

Register your lease and move-in date at the local district office to secure priority on your deposit, and check the property's registry document for existing mortgages before signing. Using a licensed agent and confirming the landlord's name matches the registry is standard advice for first-time renters.
