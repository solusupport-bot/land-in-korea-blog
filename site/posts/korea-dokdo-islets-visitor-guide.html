Visiting Dokdo: Korea's Easternmost Islets Explained

By Land in Korea Desk · Editorial policy

You can't get there directly

Dokdo sits in the Sea of Japan roughly 87 kilometers from Ulleungdo — the nearest inhabited Korean land — and about 216 kilometers from the South Korean mainland. There's no direct route from the mainland: every visitor reaches Dokdo by first getting to Ulleungdo, then taking a separate ferry from Ulleungdo's Dodong Port. If Dokdo is the goal, read our Ulleungdo guide first, since you'll be spending time there either way.

Administration, briefly

South Korea administers Dokdo as Dokdo-ri, within Ulleung-eup, Ulleung County, North Gyeongsang Province, and the islets are designated a nature reserve, which is the basis for the visitor restrictions below. Japan separately claims the islets (as part of Shimane Prefecture) and calls them Takeshima; this guide covers the practical side of visiting as South Korea administers and permits access to them, not the underlying dispute.

A landing isn't guaranteed

This is the detail that catches people off guard: getting on the boat doesn't mean you'll set foot on Dokdo. Landing is capped at roughly 70 visitors at a time, with one ferry a day, and docking depends on sea conditions — swells above roughly three to five meters make landing unsafe, and the boat simply circles instead. One older government source described landings happening as infrequently as once every 40 days, though that figure dates to 2001 and sits oddly next to more recent numbers: 2025 saw around 192,000 visitors, averaging roughly 500 a day, which only works if landings are happening far more often now than that old figure suggests. Treat the exact landing frequency as genuinely unclear from public sources, and go in expecting a real chance of a circle-only trip rather than a guaranteed landing.

What a visit actually involves

The round trip from Ulleungdo runs about four hours total, and if conditions allow a landing, you get roughly 30 minutes ashore — enough time to walk the pier, take photos, and see the handful of structures (a lighthouse, a helicopter pad, a police post) up close, not a half-day excursion. There's no town, no restaurant, no overnight stay; Dokdo has no permanent civilian residents, just a small rotating staff of lighthouse keepers, police, and administrative workers. Go in treating this as a bucket-list landing rather than a full-day activity, and the short visit won't feel like a letdown.

Booking and approval

Because of the nature reserve designation, civilian visits require advance approval through the relevant Korean authorities rather than simply buying a walk-up ticket — build this into your planning timeline rather than assuming you can decide the day before. Reported per-person costs for the tour have run in the area of 300,000+ won, though pricing, exactly which agency handles approval, and current booking channels are all worth confirming directly before you commit, since these details shift and third-party sources don't agree.

When to go

Tourist sailings to Dokdo generally run in the warmer months, with winter largely closed off by weather — if a Dokdo landing specifically (not just the boat ride) matters to your trip, aim for spring through autumn and still build in flexible days around it, since even in-season sailings get cancelled on short notice.

The short version

  • No direct route exists — every Dokdo trip starts with getting to Ulleungdo first, then a separate ferry from Dodong Port
  • Landing is capped at roughly 70 visitors at a time, once a day, and only when sea conditions allow it
  • A circle-only trip (no landing) is a real possibility — public sources disagree on exactly how often landings happen
  • A landing gives you about 30 minutes ashore within a roughly 4-hour round trip — there's nothing resembling a town
  • No permanent civilian residents — just lighthouse, police, and administrative staff
  • Civilian visits need advance approval, not a walk-up ticket — plan ahead
  • Sailings mostly run spring through autumn; winter weather largely closes the route

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I visit Dokdo directly from mainland Korea?

No. Every Dokdo trip departs from Ulleungdo's Dodong Port — there's no direct mainland-to-Dokdo ferry, so you'll need to reach Ulleungdo first.

Is landing on Dokdo guaranteed?

No. Landing depends on sea conditions and is capped at roughly 70 visitors at a time with one ferry a day. Rough swells mean the boat circles without landing — plan for this as a real possibility, not an edge case.

How long do you actually get on Dokdo?

If conditions allow a landing, about 30 minutes ashore, inside a round trip from Ulleungdo of roughly four hours total.

Does anyone live on Dokdo?

No permanent civilian residents. A small staff — lighthouse keepers, police, and administrative workers — rotates through, but there's no town or overnight accommodation.

When is the best time to visit Dokdo?

Spring through autumn, when tourist sailings generally run — winter weather largely closes the route. Even in season, build flexibility into your plan since sailings can be cancelled on short notice.
