Tipping and Service Charges in Korea: What to Actually Do

Written by Land in Korea Desk · checked against official sources — see how we work

The short answer: don't tip, but read the receipt

Korea does not have a tipping culture, and this isn't a soft guideline — at most restaurants, cafes, taxis, and salons, trying to leave extra cash on the table or hand a driver more than the fare shown can genuinely confuse the person you're trying to thank, and in some cases they'll chase you down to return it. That's the headline rule almost every visitor guide gets right. What trips people up is everything adjacent to it: hotel staff, foreign-style restaurants, and the word "service" printed on a receipt don't always mean what a visitor from a tipping country assumes.

Why tipping isn't just "optional" here

In many countries, a service worker's base wage assumes tips will make up the difference, so not tipping is a real loss to them. Korean service wages aren't structured that way — the listed price is the price, full stop, and it already reflects what the business intends to pay staff. Handing over extra cash doesn't complete a transaction the way it does elsewhere; it more often reads as either a mistake (you didn't realize the bill was already settled) or, in some framings, faintly awkward — as though you're implying the standard service wasn't already covered.

Where the "no tipping" rule holds firmly

  • Restaurants and cafes, from street-food stalls to sit-down Korean restaurants — pay exactly what's on the bill.
  • Taxis, whether hailed on the street or booked through an app — round up small change if you like, but a tip is not expected and drivers won't chase a large one.
  • Hair salons and barbershops — the listed service price is final.
  • Convenience stores and retail — obviously no tipping context here, but worth stating since some visitors default to "when in doubt, tip a little."

Where it gets genuinely confusing

Hotels

Higher-end international hotel chains in Korea sometimes follow global hotel norms more loosely than local restaurants do — a bellhop or housekeeping tip won't be refused the way a taxi tip might be, though it's still far from required. If you're staying somewhere with a strong international clientele (a five-star chain hotel, for instance), a small tip for porter service is culturally neutral rather than wrong. At a Korean-run guesthouse or mid-range hotel, it's unnecessary and slightly more likely to cause the "wait, you forgot this" moment.

The word "service" (서비스) doesn't always mean a service charge

In everyday Korean, "service" (서비스, seobiseu) very commonly means a free extra — a small side dish, a complimentary drink, a discount thrown in — not a percentage added to your bill. If a restaurant owner says "this is service," they're telling you it's free, the opposite of a tip request. This is a common point of confusion for visitors who hear "service" and reach for their wallet.

Actual service charges on the bill

Separately, a small number of upscale restaurants, hotel restaurants, and some private-room dining venues do add a genuine service charge — usually 10%, sometimes stacked with Korea's standard 10% VAT for a combined ~21% shown as a separate line. This is not a tip in the Western sense; it's a mandatory charge already built into what you owe, not a discretionary amount on top of it. Once it's on the bill, no additional tip is expected regardless of how good the service was.

If you really want to show appreciation

Tipping isn't the local way to say thanks, but there are Korea-appropriate alternatives: a genuine verbal "very delicious, thank you" (맛있었어요, 감사합니다) goes further than cash at a small restaurant, and for a guide or driver who went out of their way, a small gift rather than money is a more natural gesture in Korean service culture.

The short version

  • Korea has no tipping culture at restaurants, cafes, taxis, or salons — pay the listed price, full stop
  • "Service" (서비스) in conversation usually means a free extra, not a charge — it's the opposite of a tip request
  • A real "service charge" line on a bill (mostly upscale dining) is mandatory and already included — don't tip on top of it
  • International chain hotels are the one place a small porter tip is culturally neutral, though still not required
  • A genuine thank-you means more than cash in most everyday situations

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I tip taxi drivers in Korea?

No. Pay the metered or app-shown fare. Rounding up small change is fine if you want to, but it isn't expected and a driver won't chase you for more.

What does it mean when a restaurant owner says something is "service"?

It means it's free — a complimentary extra like a side dish or drink, not a charge. This is the opposite of what "service" implies in a tipping context, and it's one of the most common points of confusion for first-time visitors.

Is there ever a real service charge in Korea?

Yes, at some upscale restaurants, hotel restaurants, and private-room dining venues, which add roughly a 10% service charge (sometimes combined with 10% VAT for about 21% total) as a separate line on the bill. It's mandatory and already included — no additional tip is expected on top of it.

Should I tip at a hotel?

Generally not necessary. At international chain hotels, a small cash tip for a porter is culturally neutral and won't cause confusion. At Korean-run guesthouses or mid-range hotels, it's unnecessary and slightly more likely to prompt staff to return it.

Will someone be offended if I try to tip?

Not offended, but often confused — most service workers will assume you made a mistake and try to give the money back rather than accept it as a gesture.
