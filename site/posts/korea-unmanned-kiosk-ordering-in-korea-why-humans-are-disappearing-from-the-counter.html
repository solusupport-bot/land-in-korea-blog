Unmanned Kiosk Ordering in Korea - A First-Timer Guide

By Land in Korea Desk · Editorial policy

Walk into almost any fast food counter, cafe chain, or even some bakeries in Korea today and there's a good chance no one is standing behind the register. A touchscreen kiosk has taken over ordering and payment, and if you've never used one, the first attempt can feel like it's timing itself against the line of people building up behind you. It isn't actually complicated once you know where the three sticking points are: language, menu navigation, and payment.

Finding the English option

Most kiosks open on a Korean-only welcome screen, and the English toggle is usually a small flag icon, a globe symbol, or the word "ENGLISH" tucked into a top or bottom corner — easy to miss if you're expecting a full language menu up front. Large chains like Lotteria, Mom's Touch, Starbucks, Baskin Robbins, and most convenience-store-adjacent cafes have had English, Chinese, and Japanese kiosk options for years because of tourist volume. Smaller independent cafes and local restaurants are far less likely to have a translated kiosk, and some only run a Korean interface no matter what you tap.

When a kiosk genuinely has no English option, the fastest workaround is Google Translate's camera or live-view mode held over the screen — it won't translate button presses, but it reads menu names and prices well enough to know what you're ordering. Staff at small restaurants are usually used to foreigners struggling with the kiosk and will walk over and help, especially if you look lost for more than a few seconds rather than quietly giving up.

Building the order without losing your place

Korean kiosk menus are typically organized by meal category across the top (세트/단품/디저트/음료, roughly "sets/single items/dessert/drinks") with photos for every item, so even a Korean-only screen is navigable by picture alone if you already know what you want. Combo "sets" (세트) are almost always cheaper than ordering a burger, side, and drink separately, the same logic as a Western fast food value meal, and the kiosk will prompt you to pick a drink and side after you select the set.

Customization options — extra cheese, no onion, spice level — show up as a secondary screen after you tap an item, not before, so don't assume a plain listing means no modifications are possible. If you don't see a change you want, it may genuinely not be offered through the kiosk; some modifications still require asking staff directly even after you've ordered digitally.

Paying without the machine rejecting your card

This is where most first-timers actually get stuck, not the language. Korean kiosks pay by card tap, insert, or occasionally a QR/mobile pay scan directly at the machine — there is usually no option to pay a cashier in cash once you've started a kiosk order, and the kiosk itself won't take cash at all in most setups. If your card gets rejected, it's rarely the kiosk malfunctioning; the two most common causes are a magnetic-stripe-only card (increasingly rare, but still an issue for some older US cards) or your home bank's daily overseas-transaction limit kicking in after a few Seoul purchases in one day.

If a kiosk order fails right as you're trying to pay, don't panic — it almost never charges you for a failed attempt, it just cancels the order and returns you to the start screen. Try a different card, or if the location still technically has a staffed counter (increasingly rare at chains, more common at independent restaurants), step aside and ask them to ring you up manually instead.

What to do when there's no counter at all

A growing number of newer locations, especially in Seoul's busiest commercial districts, have removed the staffed counter entirely — kiosks and a pickup counter are the whole front of house. In that setup, if your card truly won't work and you only have cash, your real options are a nearby ATM (most convenience stores have one) or moving to a different restaurant that still takes cash. This is becoming less common as mobile and card payment adoption rises, but it's worth knowing before you're standing at an empty counter wondering where to hand over a 10,000 won note.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all Korean restaurant kiosks have an English option?

No. Large chains almost always do, but small independent restaurants and cafes often run Korean-only kiosks. Google Translate's camera mode over the screen is the most reliable workaround when there's no language toggle.

Can I pay cash at a kiosk in Korea?

Usually not. Most kiosks are card-only (tap, insert, or mobile pay), so if you only have cash, look for a staffed counter nearby or use a convenience-store ATM to withdraw card funds before ordering.

Why did my foreign card get rejected at a Korean kiosk?

It's rarely the kiosk itself failing. The usual causes are a magnetic-stripe-only card or your home bank's daily overseas-transaction limit after several Korean purchases — try a different card first.
