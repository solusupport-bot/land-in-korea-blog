The short version

Menus are also mostly in Korean even when the app interface offers English, so a translation app pointed at the screen is part of normal ordering. Delivery fees and minimum order amounts are set per restaurant and shown before you check out, and choosing takeout (포장) instead of delivery often skips the fee entirely.

Even with an account, the address and phone step trips people up. Riders frequently call or text to confirm a drop-off, so a number you can actually answer matters more than the app's language setting, and the free-text delivery memo is where you write things like the building name, floor, or 'leave at the door. ' Hotels and guesthouses vary — some accept deliveries at the front desk, others don't, so ask before ordering. Disposable cutlery is no longer included by default; you have to tick the option for chopsticks and spoons.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the most common first-timer mistake with food delivery apps in korea: why signing up is harder than ordering?

Korea's delivery ecosystem runs on a few big apps — Baemin, Coupang Eats, and Yogiyo — and the food itself arrives fast and cheap by global standards.

What should I know before I go?

The hard part is account setup: most apps expect a Korean mobile number for verification, and payment usually flows through a Korean-issued card or a domestic wallet like Kakao Pay or Naver Pay, though foreign cards work in some cases.

Any quick tip to remember?

Delivery to outdoor spots like Han River parks is a local ritual, and it works by choosing a park-zone address and meeting the rider at a designated pickup point rather than at your picnic mat.
