Working From Cafes in Korea: Study Cafes vs. Chains

By Land in Korea Desk · Editorial policy

What matters here

Independent dessert cafes and small photogenic spots often post signs — sometimes with a crossed-out laptop icon — banning laptops entirely or on weekends, because their seats turn over on drink sales. Reading the sticker on the door before you settle in saves an awkward conversation.

If you genuinely need to work, a 스터디카페 (study cafe) is the purpose-built option: you pay by the hour or buy a time block at a kiosk, get an assigned seat or private booth, and there's silence, free drip coffee or water, and reliable Wi-Fi. Many are unstaffed and run entirely on touchscreen check-in, with door codes issued by the machine. Silence is strictly enforced — phone calls happen in the hallway, and typing loudly can earn you a glare.

Reading the signs on the door

Korean cafes signal their rules visually more often than they explain them. A crossed-out laptop icon means no laptops at all, sometimes limited to weekend or rush-hour windows only. 1인 1메뉴 ("one item per person") is a common house rule at busy cafes — it means every person at the table has to order something, not just one drink for two people splitting it, and staff will ask if they notice you skipping it. Some cafes also post a time limit, often "2시간" (2 hours) near the register, especially on weekends when turnover matters more. Outlet access is its own signal: a cafe that's proud of its plugs will say so ("콘센트 많아요", lots of outlets), while one trying to discourage laptop squatters sometimes removes visible outlets near window seating entirely rather than post a sign.

How study cafes actually price their time

Most study cafes run on one of three structures: a straight hourly rate paid at the kiosk, a discounted multi-hour or all-day block bought upfront, or a monthly pass aimed at students and remote workers who show up daily. The all-day and monthly options work out meaningfully cheaper per hour than paying hourly each visit, so it's worth checking the kiosk's full price list rather than defaulting to the first hourly rate shown. Finding one is straightforward even without Korean: search "study cafe" or "스터디카페" directly in Naver Map, which will show nearby locations along with user photos of the seating and booth layout, so you can judge the vibe before walking over.

Coworking spaces as a longer-stay alternative

For a stay longer than a few days, a study cafe's hourly model starts adding up. Coworking chains — WeWork, Fastfive, and Spacemob all operate multiple locations in Seoul — sell day passes and short-term memberships aimed at exactly this gap between a cafe and a full office lease, with meeting rooms, proper desks, and none of the one-drink etiquette. Most require booking a day pass online rather than walking in, and availability varies by location, so check the specific branch's app or site before showing up.

Wifi speed generally isn't the bottleneck

Korea's internet infrastructure is fast almost everywhere, so a slow connection is rarely the reason a cafe doesn't work for a video call or a large upload — the real variables are noise, seating turnover pressure, and whether the space was designed for lingering in the first place. Chain cafes with bar-height counters along the window are usually built for solo laptop use specifically, while cafes with mostly small round tables and soft seating are signaling a different kind of visit, regardless of what their wifi speed actually is.

Peak hours are predictable

Franchise chains near university campuses and office districts fill up fastest on weekday afternoons and weekend mornings, when students and freelancers settle in for long stretches. Arriving right after a chain opens, or working through the early evening dinner lull, is usually the easiest way to find a window seat without circling the room with a tray in hand.
