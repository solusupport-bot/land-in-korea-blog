Seoul City Buses Explained by Color, Stop and Tapping Off

Written by Land in Korea Desk · checked against official sources — see how we work

Standing politely at a Korean bus stop is one of the easiest ways to miss your bus. Drivers in Korean cities don't stop at every stop by default, and if nobody at the curb looks like they want that particular bus, it will often roll straight past. City buses here are fast, frequent, and cheap, but they reward a more assertive rider than the subway does. Here's what actually matters once you're off the train and onto the street.

What the bus colors in Seoul actually tell you

Seoul's city buses are color-coded, and the colors say something about how each bus behaves:

  • Blue buses run long trunk routes across the city, usually along major roads and often in the center-lane bus corridors.
  • Green buses are shorter neighborhood feeders that link residential streets to subway stations.
  • Yellow buses circulate within a small downtown area on a loop.
  • Red buses are express routes that run out to the Gyeonggi suburbs with widely spaced stops.

Fares follow the colors too. According to Seoul's official fare table (in effect since August 12, 2023), an adult ride on a blue or green bus costs 1,500 won, a yellow circular bus 1,400 won, and a red express bus 3,000 won, with the same adult price whether you pay by card or cash. The base fare covers the first 10 km of a trip, including any transfers (30 km if a red express bus is involved), and longer journeys add 100 won per extra 5 km. Red buses are also different in another way: because many of them use expressways, they're bound by the rule that every passenger must be seated with a seatbelt on. When all the seats are taken, the bus stops boarding, which is why commuters at big red-bus stops check how many seats are left before the bus arrives. For many Gyeonggi express routes, Kakao Map and the KakaoBus app show remaining seats alongside the arrival time.

Don't carry Seoul's color logic to other cities. Busan, Daegu, and Incheon each run their own bus systems with their own route types and numbering. Treat the route number, checked in a map app, as the only reliable identifier anywhere in the country.

Finding the right stop, on the right side of the road

This is where most first bus rides go wrong. Korean bus stops usually carry the same name on both sides of a road, one for each direction. Before you wait, look at the stop sign or in your app for the next stop listed for your bus. If the next stop is the one you just came from, you're on the wrong side.

Seoul adds a twist with its center-lane bus corridors. On many major roads, blue buses run in dedicated lanes in the middle of the road, and the stops are islands between the traffic lanes, reached by a crosswalk. If you're standing on the sidewalk waiting for a bus your app says is "arriving," look toward the middle of the road.

Each stop also has a five-digit stop number on its sign. Typing that number into Naver Map or Kakao Map pulls up that exact platform, which is more reliable than searching by name when several stops share one. Many stops show live arrival times on a screen too, and the countdown usually matches what the apps show.

Why you have to flag the bus down

When your bus appears, step toward the curb and raise your hand as it approaches. At busy stops several buses may pull in at once, stopping behind each other rather than at the sign, so be ready to walk a few meters to your bus's door. If the driver sees nobody moving toward the curb, the bus may not stop at all.

Getting off works the same way. The bus won't stop unless someone presses a stop button, found on the poles and near the windows, before the stop. Onboard announcements and screens name each stop in Korean, with English on many routes, but it helps to follow your position on the map app so you're ready.

Drivers also pull away quickly, both from the stop and from the curb. Start moving toward the rear door as you approach your stop, holding the poles or straps, and press the button early. Waiting in your seat until the bus is fully stopped can mean the doors close before you reach them. You board at the front and get off through the rear door.

Tap off, every single time

The single habit that costs foreigners the most money is skipping the tap when getting off. There is a card reader by the rear door, and you should tap your card on it every time you leave the bus, even on a flat-fare route where tapping off doesn't seem to change anything. If you haven't picked up a card yet, our T-money card guide covers buying and loading one.

Here's why. Free transfers between buses, and between buses and the subway, only work when both the boarding tap and the exit tap are recorded. In Seoul you get up to four free transfers, which is five rides in total, as long as you board the next vehicle within 30 minutes of tapping off. Between 9 p.m. and 7 a.m. that window stretches to one hour. Transferring to another bus with the same route number doesn't count as a transfer. If you don't tap off, the system can't see where you left, so your next bus or subway ride starts from zero and is charged a full new fare. On longer trips, the distance charge is calculated from those same taps.

When a transfer registers, the reader announces 환승입니다 (hwanseung-imnida, "this is a transfer"), which is a handy confirmation that you did it right. (The subway has also charged riders who skip the exit tap an extra base fare on their next trip since March 2026.)

A final payment detail: a growing number of Seoul buses no longer accept cash at all, and cash fares never earn the transfer discount anyway. Keep a loaded transit card on you rather than counting on coins.

Korean words you'll see and hear on the bus

Stop screens, onboard displays, and card readers mix Korean and English, and the Korean parts are often the most useful. These are the ones worth recognizing:

  • 정류장 / 정류소 (jeongnyujang / jeongnyuso): bus stop.
  • 이번 정류장 (ibeon jeongnyujang): "this stop," the one the bus is approaching now. 다음 정류장 (daeum jeongnyujang) is the one after it.
  • 곧 도착 (got dochak): "arriving soon" on the countdown screen at the stop.
  • 승차 / 하차 (seungcha / hacha): boarding and getting off. The rear door and its reader are the 하차 side.
  • 저상버스 (jeosang beoseu): low-floor bus, flagged on arrival screens. Handy with a stroller or heavy suitcase.
  • 카드를 한 장만 대주세요: "please tap only one card." The reader says this when it detects two cards in your wallet, and the tap fails.
  • 잔액이 부족합니다: "insufficient balance." Top up at a convenience store before your next ride.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I tap a foreign Visa or Mastercard contactless card on Seoul buses?

Not yet. Seoul is fitting buses with EMV-certified readers so overseas cards can be tapped directly, with bus service expected around 2027; until then, subway station kiosks accept overseas cards for single-journey tickets and for buying or charging a Climate Card (including short-term tourist passes).

Is there a cheaper time of day to ride the bus in Seoul?

Yes. If you pay with a transit card and board before 6:30 a.m., the base fare of your first ride gets a 20% early-morning discount, so a 1,500-won blue or green bus ride costs 1,200 won.

How much does a Seoul village bus cost compared with a regular city bus?

Village buses (maeul buses), the small green buses that serve narrow neighborhood streets, cost 1,200 won for adults, less than the 1,500 won on regular blue and green buses. They join the same transfer system, so tapping off still matters.
