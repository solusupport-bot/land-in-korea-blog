"What Korean Convenience Stores Actually Do Besides Sell Snacks"

CU, GS25, and 7-Eleven are open 24/7 almost everywhere in Korea, and most tourists treat them as a snack stop and nothing more. That's a fraction of what they're actually built to do.

Which chain is which

CU and GS25 are the two biggest chains by store count, 7-Eleven is close behind, and Emart24 is a smaller but still common fourth option — functionally, all four do roughly the same things described below, so which one you walk into usually just comes down to which is closest, not which is "better."

The cheapest breakfast in Korea isn't a restaurant

A convenience store coffee and a toast or sandwich combo is a fast, genuinely cheap breakfast option for the early mornings when nothing else nearby is open. Most locations also have microwaves and hot water at the counter or a small seating area, so instant noodles and heat-and-eat meals are an actual sit-down option, not just a snack. Many stores also run a warmer case at the counter with rotating hot items like corn dogs and fried snacks — worth a look even if you weren't planning on eating there.

Mini pharmacy, ATM, delivery counter, and transit top-up, all in one

Many locations sell single-dose medicine (painkillers, cold medicine) — useful given how limited Korean pharmacy hours can be, though selection is basic. Most have an ATM (a foreign-card withdrawal fee usually applies — check your banking app rather than assuming it's free). Some counters also process bill payments, concert or show ticket purchases, and parcel shipping — including, at some locations, drop-off points for luggage forwarding services, which is worth knowing if you're trying to send bags ahead rather than lugging them between cities yourself. The same counter is also where you top up a T-money transit card balance, and the card itself can be used to pay for small purchases in the store — the two are more connected than most visitors realize.

Alcohol, and yes, a photocopier

Convenience stores in Korea also sell beer, soju, and other alcohol off the shelf (with an ID check for anyone who looks like they might be under the legal drinking age), which surprises visitors from places where alcohol sales are restricted to dedicated liquor stores. Many locations also have a self-service printer/copier machine tucked in a corner — handy in a pinch for printing a boarding pass or a reservation confirmation.

The discount stickers worth actually checking

Look for 1+1 or 2+1 stickers, especially on drinks and snacks — these are real bundle discounts, not a pricing trick, and they add up over a week-long trip if you're grabbing drinks and snacks daily anyway.

Worth browsing even if you're not hungry

Korean convenience stores are known for frequent limited-edition and collaboration snacks that rotate in and out fast, which is part of why some travelers specifically browse a few different stores just to see what's currently on shelves — it's a small, low-cost way to get a slice of local pop culture that has nothing to do with sightseeing.

When it's genuinely the better option over a restaurant

Late at night, between attractions with no real restaurant nearby, or on a travel day when you don't want to sit down for a full meal — a convenience store meal isn't a compromise in Korea the way it might be elsewhere. The hot-food counter selection is a legitimate, fast option, not just a backup.

How to actually use one without fumbling at the counter

The layout is consistent enough across chains that learning it once covers the whole trip:

  • Hot water and microwaves are self-service, usually on a counter near the seating area rather than behind the till. Cup noodles have a fill line inside; chopsticks, lids, and disposable spoons sit next to the dispenser, and there's a bin for the empty cup when you're done.
  • Ask before you heat. Boxed meals and packaged rice need to be microwaved, and the staff will usually do it if you hold the item up and gesture toward the microwave — no shared language required.
  • Bags aren't automatic. Korea restricts free plastic bags at retail, so expect to be asked whether you want one and to pay a small amount for it. Carrying a foldable tote saves that small friction several times a day.
  • Paying is fast. Tap-to-pay works on most foreign contactless cards, and a T-money card with a balance also works for small purchases. Cash is fine everywhere; tipping is not a thing here any more than anywhere else in Korea.
  • Seating isn't guaranteed. Some branches have a proper counter with stools, others have nothing at all. If you're planning to eat in, glance through the window for seating before committing to a hot meal.

The short version

  • CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, and Emart24 are functionally interchangeable — pick whichever is closest
  • Hot water, microwaves, and a warmer case make it a real meal option, not just snacks
  • ATMs, bill payment, parcel/luggage forwarding drop-off, and T-money top-ups are all available at the counter
  • Alcohol is sold off the shelf, and many locations have a self-service printer/copier
  • 1+1 and 2+1 stickers are genuine bundle discounts worth checking

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get breakfast at a Korean convenience store?

Yes — a coffee and toast or sandwich combo is a fast, cheap breakfast option, and many locations have microwaves, hot water, and a hot-food warmer case for heat-and-eat meals.

Do Korean convenience stores sell medicine?

Many sell single-dose painkillers and cold medicine, useful given how limited Korean pharmacy hours can be, though selection is basic.

Can I top up my T-money card at a convenience store?

Yes — the same counter that handles bill payments and parcel shipping is also where you top up a T-money balance, and the card can be used to pay for purchases in the store itself.

Do convenience stores sell alcohol in Korea?

Yes — beer, soju, and other alcohol are sold off the shelf, with an ID check applied the same way it would be at a dedicated liquor store elsewhere.

Are the discount stickers on drinks and snacks real deals?

Yes — 1+1 and 2+1 stickers are genuine bundle discounts, not a pricing trick, and they add up over a week-long trip.

Is there a difference between CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, and Emart24?

Not functionally, for a traveler's purposes — all four offer roughly the same services, so proximity matters more than brand.
